Assessed value vs. effective market value: the number that decides a market-value appeal

Every January, the NYC Department of Finance mails your Notice of Property Value. It shows two numbers that are easy to mix up: market value and assessed value. Here is what each one means for a Tax Class 1 home (one to three families), and which one decides an appeal that challenges the Department of Finance's market value.

  • Market value: what the Department of Finance estimates your home would sell for, based on sales of similar homes in similar neighborhoods.
  • Assessed value: your market value multiplied by the 6% Class 1 assessment ratio, limited by state caps.
  • The caps: by law, a Class 1 assessed value cannot rise more than 6% in one year or 20% over five years, aside from physical changes to the property.
  • Effective market value: what you are actually being taxed on. Because of the caps, your assessed value is often less than 6% of your market value. The Department of Finance calculates the effective market value as your capped assessed value divided by 6%.

The claim this decides

If you challenge the Department of Finance's market value (an excessive-assessment claim), the NYC Tax Commission lowers your value only if you prove your home is worth less than its effective market value. Showing your home is worth less than the market value printed on your notice is not enough. Other grounds, such as unequal assessment or misclassification, follow different rules.

A hypothetical example

Say your notice shows a market value of $850,000, but years of caps have left your effective market value at $700,000. Comparable sales at $800,000 do not help, because you are already being taxed on less than that. You would need credible sales below $700,000. That is why we check the effective number first, before telling you whether a market-value case is worth filing.
